For six years, at home, I was more or less the official handywoman. Plumber, gardener, decorator, organiser, logistics, shopping, ideas… if there was something to be done, I'd probably already thought of it before anyone else. And I'm not saying that to dump it all on Marcos. In fact, he was surely delighted to have someone who solved things before the problem even existed. I was delighted too. My initiative came naturally, and since it didn't cost me much, I kept doing more and more of it.

That said: he got comfortable too. Not because I was some tyrant who wouldn't let him do anything —although it could also be that I was a bit, I won't lie to you— but because when one person is always a step ahead, the other can end up thinking: "Perfect, Violeta's got that under control."

And so, without really noticing, we built ourselves a dynamic.

Our great culinary moment, for example, was making homemade pizza. And when I say homemade I mean we bought the dough, threw a few things on top and stuck it in the oven. He'd always told me he loved cooking and, in fact, he has a family restaurant, but in six years I never quite saw that overflowing culinary passion I'd been expecting. I, on the other hand, can be very practical: with an egg in the microwave and a bit of lettuce I can call dinner sorted and go to bed with a clear conscience.

The house, now, that was another matter. We'd designed that house together, but I put an enormous amount of initiative into it. I decorated it to my taste, I thought through the spaces, the details, the things I wanted in it… and I left a lot of myself in there.

Which is why, when I later found out that Marcos had a partner and found out that he still had our paintings up, I thought: "Blimey, but decorate it differently…"

He said to me:

"You know I'm no good at all at that, Violeta."

And I laughed on the inside.

Poor girl.

I'd have a fit.

But that moment also helped me understand my old life a bit better. I was very used to being at the wheel. And at the time, it didn't even feel heavy to me. It was simply how I worked.

In fact, even after we separated, I discovered that this need to look after people, to understand, to put everything in its place, still came out in me.

I remember a village festival where Marcos, his new partner, me and the whole gang all ended up together. I was still working out how I felt in that new life and, surprisingly, seeing them together didn't set off the Greek tragedy I might have imagined months earlier. I saw them right there, close by, and I smiled and watched the situation with a calm that surprised even me.

Deep down, I'd loved Marcos so much that all I wanted was for him to be all right. And if it wasn't going to be with me, then I was glad he could find it with someone else.

Mind you, I was deep in my happy flowers phase, and that has its dangers too.

I ran into a friend of his and, with both my ovaries, I told her that if Martina felt uncomfortable going to Marcos's house, I understood completely. But that I hoped she'd end up really enjoying that house, because it had been made with a lot of love.

All very lovely.

Very evolved.

Very "I just want you to be happy".

Until Alex hugged me and asked:

"What were you saying to her?"

And I hugged her.

And I started crying my eyes out.

Because being emotionally evolved is one thing, and someone asking you the right question at the right moment is quite another.

That night I slept at Alex's.

Thank you, Alex. I'll remember that for the rest of my life.

I do love playing the brave one. What need do I have to be Mother Teresa of Calcutta?

And maybe that's why, when Max turned up, there was something that particularly caught my attention.

Because he didn't work the way I was used to.

Max had initiative. Loads of it. In every sense. He was one of those people who suggest things, who organise, who do things, who have plans, who know what they want to do and who, if they fancy something, simply do it.

And I was blown away by that.

Not because I needed someone to take the wheel for me. Precisely because, after spending so long doing the driving myself, discovering that I could sit beside someone who also knew how to take it turned out to be far more attractive than I would have imagined.

Those first weeks, my parents were key. I needed to talk. A lot. I process by thinking out loud; getting things out of me puts my head in order. So I'd grab them and take them out walking. Sometimes twice a day. Poor things.

With my mother I talked about everything. There were moments when I gave her so much information that she reached a point where she didn't even know what to say to me. And then she'd start telling me her own stories, especially the ones about that break-up she'd been through before meeting my father, more than thirty years ago. And I listened to her thinking that, in the end, maybe break-ups change their scenery but there are quite a few things in them that repeat.

My father, on the other hand, took exactly two days to hand me a solution.

"If I were you, I'd download a dating app and do some serious screening at the start."

I looked at him thinking he'd gone mad.

A dating app? Me? After a six-year relationship?

Well then.

In the end I did exactly that.

It really amuses me to think how alike we are, because that man I thought had gone a bit mad on day two had actually understood me pretty well.

And then there were my friends, who were another fundamental piece of those months.

Bruna and Olívia were part of my daily life almost constantly. We shared a WhatsApp group, we made lots of plans and, for a while, practically every afternoon we saw each other, talked, did whatever, and I went home a little bit lighter.

Alex was always available for a good conversation. Whether she was living nearby or I had to travel to see her when she lived away, there was always room for me. And as if that weren't enough, she introduced me to her friends and kept opening doors for me to new people and new places.

With Sofia I've always felt very comfortable too, even though she lived far away. That Sant Joan, not long after the break-up, I went to Italy with her and felt Italian within a matter of hours. She fitted me perfectly into her life and, on top of that, she was also going through a complicated moment with her partner. We understood each other pretty well because, one way or another, we were both trying to work out what on earth we were doing with our lives.

And then there was Juliana.

She and I have always been very close, but at that moment her life was practically the antithesis of mine. She was engaged, pregnant and had just built a house with Nacho. And I was delighted for her, because I knew it was exactly what she'd always wanted, and in that order too. It made me so happy to see her fulfilling her life plan.

I remember one day when, bless her, she was telling me all excited how happy she was and, in the middle of the conversation, she said to me:

"Gosh, I'm sorry for showing so much happiness."

Excuse me?

"You're inspiring me! If you've got it, I can have it too. Enjoy it, Sofia."

She didn't know yet that she was pregnant.

Not long after, she told us.

And it was incredible.

And I think that also explains those five months a little. I was broken, yes, but I wasn't alone. I had people around me who listened to me, who made me laugh, who opened doors for me, who let me cry and who, above all, constantly reminded me that life carried on.

And that it could take many shapes.

Some of them I wanted.

Others I didn't even know existed yet.
